# Fable Writing

## Objective

Write one fable around a single concept and explain it fully. Speak indirectly, as a true fable does—do not state the concept outright. Do not use concept names or disciplinary terminology in the body.

Ideally, the reader should only gradually realize what the concept is as the story nears its end. Then, after the story, add an explanation that clearly states what you were really trying to convey.

The story should be publication-ready: independently readable, with a complete situation, character actions, and a turning point. The explanation should be accurate, clear, and trace every point back to a story image.

## Workflow

1. Confirm the concept, target reader, chapter position, relationships with preceding and following chapters, and whether this task is new writing, continuation, or revision.
2. Extract the concept name, English name, terminology, and prerequisite/successor relationships from user instructions, project materials, the concept map, the writing plan, or the original draft.
3. First find a concrete story device that can carry the conceptual relationship: an object, a place, a profession, a rule, a ritual, a dispute, a measurement, a record, a repair, a voyage, a trial, etc.
4. When writing the body, let the problem arise first, then have characters act, then let familiar methods fail, and finally bring the reader to the edge of the concept—but do not name it prematurely in the body.
5. When writing the explanation, first map the core metaphor to the real concept, then list points mapping story images to the concept, and finally explain that concept’s significance in the overall book arc.
6. When revising an old draft, first diagnose failure points: structure, character motivation, metaphor discipline, conceptual accuracy, language clarity, pacing, reader fit, or continuity. Unless the user asks for a full rewrite, prefer the minimum change that solves a real problem.
7. After completion, check terminology, title formatting, image references, cross-chapter continuity, and project rules.

## Pre-Writing Association

Even when using this skill alone, first find a well-known novel, film, play, or myth that can serve as a reference for the target concept.

Prioritize accessible, plot-driven works that require no extra background knowledge. Science fiction is ideal (e.g., *The Three-Body Problem*, *Arrival*). Avoid obscure classics, works that rely mainly on abstract symbolism, or works that require understanding complex intellectual backgrounds. Whether a work is "famous" matters less than whether readers can quickly recall the relevant plot.

Execution order:
- Identify famous plots related to the target concept.
- Judge whether this plot helps readers relate to the concept or distracts from it.
- Make necessary original transformations; do not mechanically copy the original work. Retain recognizable elements that serve conceptual understanding.


## Explanation Section Format

Use the following format for each concept unit unless the user or project files explicitly require a different format:

```markdown
### The Truth Behind the Fable: Concept Chinese Name (English Name)

The "core image" in the story corresponds to …

Here are the conceptual points this fable maps to:

- **Story image (conceptual point)**: Start from a concrete object, action, or conflict in the story, then map to the real concept.

Therefore, what this fable is really about: …
```

- The concept Chinese name and English name in the title must come from source materials; do not invent an English name if the source lacks one.
- The first paragraph must map the story’s core metaphor to the real concept.
- The bullet list must start from story images, not from encyclopedic concept categories.
- Each point answers: "What does this story detail correspond to in the concept?"
- Necessary terminology and symbols may be retained, but explain them immediately in plain language.
- The closing paragraph should explain the concept’s significance in the book’s arc and naturally lead toward the next concept or chapter.

## Language Standards

- Plain, concrete, drawable. Prioritize people, objects, actions, locations, time, and consequences.
- Short sentences preferred, clear subject. Each sentence should ideally perform one technical action or one narrative action.
- Sound like a calm person telling a story, not like an abstract summary.
- fable-like, but not childish; mild humor is acceptable, but do not let cleverness overshadow the concept.
- Do not use harder-to-imagine sentences to create a sense of "depth." Beauty must serve clarity.
- The explanation should be accurate, natural, and pedagogically clear; do not drift into generic encyclopedia entries.
- No AI aftertaste.

Sentence test:

> Can a diligent middle-school student understand this on the first reading?

Image test:

> Can an illustrator draw a picture from this sentence?

## Terminology and Naming Discipline

- Do not invent concept words, Chinese translations, English names, or abbreviations on your own. Concept names must come from user instructions, project materials, the concept map, the writing plan, or the original draft.
- Do not write jargon, pseudo-terminology, or slogan-like abstract words, especially do not package ordinary meanings as "some mechanism," "some law," or "some system."
- The fable body may create concrete objects and scene images, but these must feel like things from a real story: lamps, scales, doors, ledgers, wells, bridges. Do not create metaphors that sound like theoretical terms.
- The concept Chinese name and English name in the explanation title must use names already present in the source materials; if the source lacks an English name, do not invent one.
- If you must temporarily name a place, object, or organization in the story, the name should be plain, short, and understandable, and used only as a story object, not as an academic term.
- When revising an old draft, prefer reusing characters, objects, places, and terminology already established in the draft and project; do not replace them with unfamiliar jargon for the sake of "freshness."

## Revision Rules

- Preserve useful existing images; deepen, clarify, or adjust them first. Do not lightly swap in an entirely new metaphor set.
- Do not move technical concepts into the story body to solve an explanation problem; make the story more visible instead, and explain clearly in the explanation section.
- When modifying a single sentence, check the preceding and following sentences to ensure the replacement connects naturally.
- When revising an entire chapter, maintain causal continuity: changes should affect what characters see, choose, fear, misunderstand, or do next.
- When fixing continuity, solve it inside the story world; do not use authorial commentary to patch holes.
- A good fix should feel like the story was always meant to be written this way. If a new sentence reads like a response to editorial feedback, rewrite it as an action, a memory, an object, a hesitation, or a habit.

### Continuity Fixes

When encountering contradictions, broken foreshadowing, abrupt transitions, or dangling images:

1. First identify what is broken: timeline, character identity, foreshadowing, causal consequence, chapter transition, or concept mapping.
2. Choose the least conspicuous patch point: usually an early setup, the first later mention, or a small bridge between two paragraphs.
3. Turn explanation into story evidence. Do not write "This happened before she became a teacher"; instead, have her enter the next scene carrying an object, habit, or memory.
4. Let concrete objects or actions carry continuity: lamps, rulers, maps, ledgers, scars, promises, questions, old rules, unopened doors.
5. Reread both sides of the patch. The preceding sentence should invite it; the following sentence should no longer need extra explanation.

Avoid the following patch sentence patterns:

- "This echoes the previous chapter…"
- "The author intends to express…"
- "This will be explained later…"
- "At this point he had not yet…"
- Any sentence whose main function is merely plugging a hole without advancing the story.

## Common Rewrite Operations

- Turn abstract judgments into visible work.
  - Weak: "Knowledge grows through critical examination."
  - Strong: "She filed away the failure records and deleted the old rule that always led people astray."
- Turn thesis-style openings into scene openings.
  - Weak: "The demarcation problem discusses the distinction between science and pseudoscience."
  - Strong: "The town held a market at the start of every month. The busiest stalls were always the ones that spun bad news into good."
- Turn decorative depth into usable images.
  - Weak: "Truth reveals its boundaries in the light of experience."
  - Strong: "That tool did not merely help him speak; it could also stop him at certain times."
- Turn continuity patches into carried objects.
  - Weak: "This echoes the theme of the previous chapter."
  - Strong: "She pocketed the small object left from the previous chapter and carried it into the new scene."
- Turn "prettier" sentences into clearer causality.
  - Weak: "The tides of fate wash against the banks of cognition."
  - Strong: "When the cloth was lifted, the shortcut that had been praised revealed exactly where it could go wrong."

## Sentence Polishing

- Break apart nested long sentences.
- Reduce "in… within…" chains; rewrite as clear subject-verb-object.
- Avoid stacking abstract verbs like "evaluate," "produce," "form," "drive," "realize," "present" in the body.
- When the second half of "not only… but also…" grows too long, split into two sentences.
- When quoted speech grows too long, move part of it outside the quotation marks.
- In the explanation section, retain terminology but flatten sentences. When encountering dense definitions, use "that is to say," "in other words," or a short clarifying sentence.
- Each explanation sentence should perform one technical action. After abstract terms, return to story images as much as possible.

## Banned Tropes

### Imagery

Avoid: clocks, rivers, mirrors, labyrinths, looms, maps, lighthouses, chessboards, echoes, shadows, hourglasses, wind, candles, seeds, bridges, stars, butterflies, spiderwebs.

### Locations

Do not invent overwrought literary place names like "Echo City," "Village of Memory," "Sea of Forgetting," or "Silent Valley." Use ordinary place names, or do not name them at all.

### Structures

- Traveler seeks wisdom from a sage.
- Strange phenomenon in a village → collective epiphany.
- A child’s single sentence awakens the adults.
- Master–disciple dialectical debate.
- Deathbed last words.

### Characters

Clockmaker, librarian, hermit, storyteller, old ferryman, brewer, blacksmith, scribe.

### Openings

Do not begin with "Once upon a time in a certain place…" "One day someone encountered something…" or "In a distant mountain…" Drop directly into the scene.

## Pre-Completion Checklist

- Does the story body name the concept or use disciplinary terminology too early?
- Can the core sentences be drawn as pictures?
- Are character actions, misunderstandings, turning points, and consequences concrete enough?
- Does the explanation section first map the core metaphor, then list points?
- Can every point trace back to a specific story detail?
- Do concept names, English names, symbols, and terminology come from reliable sources?
- Does the chapter ending naturally lead toward the next concept or question?
- Does the revised draft preserve original characters, objects, places, and cross-chapter continuity?
